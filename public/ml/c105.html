C’est la question que Karl Pearson se pose en 1900. Il compare, catégorie par catégorie, ce qu’il observe et ce que le modèle prévoit. Le chapitre 106 s’en servira pour trancher.

This is the question Karl Pearson asks himself in 1900. He compares, category by category, what he observes and what the model predicts. He scales each gap, squares it, and adds them up. To judge the result he needs the distribution of a sum of squares. The German geodesist Friedrich Robert Helmert had already met it in 1876, while studying measurement errors. Today it bears the name Pearson gave to his statistic: the chi-square, written 𝜒2 (𝜒 is the Greek letter “chi”). The small 2 is part of the name: it recalls that squares are added up. We never compute a “𝜒” to be multiplied by itself. This chapter builds that distribution. Chapter 106 will use it to decide.

We give a name to the distribution of this sum: the chi-square distribution, and the number of squares is called its degrees of freedom.
